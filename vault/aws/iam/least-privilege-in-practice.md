# Least Privilege in Practice

Every note in this category has been building toward the same practical
question: given all these mechanisms for granting, capping, and
conditioning access, how does a real team actually end up with policies
that grant no more than what's needed — and why do teams so reliably
fail to?

**Wildcards accumulate.** `s3:*` on `Resource: "*"` is the fastest way to
unblock a developer who's hit an Access Denied, and it works every time
— which is exactly the problem. Each individual wildcard grant is a
rational response to an immediate blocker, but nothing about IAM
prompts anyone to come back later and narrow it once the blocker is
gone; the ==default trajectory== of a policy under normal development ^card-vraf
pressure is to only ever get broader, never narrower, unless something
deliberately intervenes.

> [!card] mcq
> Why do overly broad IAM policies tend to accumulate over a project's
> lifetime rather than staying narrow from the start?
> - [x] Widening a policy immediately unblocks whoever hit the denial, while narrowing one requires someone to notice the excess and deliberately do the (riskier, lower-urgency) work of tightening it
> - [ ] IAM policies automatically widen their own scope over time as a platform behavior
> - [ ] Least privilege is a theoretical ideal that AWS's own tooling actively discourages
> - [ ] Wildcards are required for any policy attached to a role, unlike one attached to a user ^card-jrpv

Why is "grant broad access now, narrow it later" a much easier habit to fall into than to reverse? :: Granting broad access resolves an immediate, visible problem (a blocked developer, a failing deploy) with a one-line change; narrowing it later requires someone to first notice the excess exists, then verify narrowing it won't break something that quietly depends on it — work that's invisible until it's skipped, and easy to keep deferring because nothing forces it to happen. ^card-aciq

**IAM Access Analyzer** attacks this from the other direction: instead of
guessing at the narrowest policy a role could need, it observes the
role's actual CloudTrail activity over a chosen window and generates a
policy scoped to the ==actions and resources that were genuinely used== — ^card-7tzw
turning "what does this role need" from a manual audit of application
code into an evidence-based question the platform can answer for you.
Access Analyzer's policy generation is a starting point to review, not
something to accept unmodified — activity logs only capture what
happened to run during the observation window, not every legitimate
path through the code.

> [!card] recall
> Explain why a policy generated by Access Analyzer from 30 days of
> observed activity might be *too* narrow for a role that runs
> infrequent, but legitimate, code paths — a monthly batch job, an
> error-handling branch that's never actually been triggered. What risk
> does blindly adopting the generated policy carry that a more
> conservative "review and adjust" approach avoids? ^card-qpu8

There is a real, honest tension here, not a puzzle with a clean answer:
tightening a policy risks breaking something that currently works, and
verifying it won't requires either exhaustive test coverage of every
code path that touches AWS or a long, careful observation window before
narrowing anything — both of which cost time a team under deadline
pressure often doesn't feel it has. Shipping wins that tradeoff far more
often than security does, which is precisely why wildcards accumulate
in the first place rather than despite anyone's intentions.

What makes "just tighten the policy" a genuinely harder recommendation to follow than it sounds, rather than simple negligence on a team's part? :: Tightening requires confidence that nothing still legitimately depends on the permissions being removed, and that confidence is expensive to obtain — it needs either thorough monitoring over enough time to see rare code paths exercise their permissions, or enough test coverage to exercise them deliberately; without that confidence, narrowing a policy is a bet that something currently working keeps working, made under exactly the same time pressure that produced the wildcard in the first place. ^card-qftr

Least privilege, in practice, is not a state a policy reaches once and
keeps — it's an ongoing process of periodically re-examining what a role
still actually uses, made tractable by tools like Access Analyzer and
made necessary by the fact that permissions granted under pressure
almost never get revisited under equal pressure to remove them.

Why is "least privilege" better understood as a continuous process than a one-time policy-writing exercise? :: Because the forces that widen a policy (an urgent unblock) recur constantly while the forces that narrow one (a deliberate audit) don't happen on their own — treating least privilege as something achieved once at policy-authoring time ignores that the same accumulation pressure keeps acting on the policy for as long as the role exists, so only a recurring review process actually counteracts it. ^card-5k0o
