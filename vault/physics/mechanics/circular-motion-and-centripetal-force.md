# Circular motion and centripetal force

The previous three notes in this shelf all analyze straight-line and
incline motion. This one covers motion along a circle, and it exists to
correct a specific misconception baked into its own vocabulary: there is
no such thing as a centripetal force *in addition to* gravity, tension,
friction and normal force — "centripetal" just names the role one of
those familiar forces is playing.

In uniform circular motion, speed is constant but velocity is not,
because velocity is a vector and direction is constantly changing. A
changing velocity means there is an acceleration, even though speed never
changes — and that acceleration points toward the center of the circle,
which is why it's called centripetal ("center-seeking") acceleration.

```
a_c = v^2/r         (v = speed, r = radius)
a_c = omega^2*r      (omega = angular velocity, rad/s)
```

> [!card] recall
> A car moves around a circular track at constant speed. Explain why it
> has a nonzero acceleration despite the speed never changing, and give
> the two equivalent formulas for the magnitude of that acceleration.
> ---
> Acceleration measures the rate of change of velocity, a vector, and
> velocity changes whenever direction changes — even at constant speed,
> the car's direction is changing continuously as it goes around the
> curve, so it has nonzero acceleration. That acceleration points toward
> the circle's center, with magnitude a_c = v^2/r = omega^2*r. ^card-kajx

This is the note's central point: **centripetal force is not a fifth
fundamental force.** It is simply the name given to whatever net force
happens to point toward the center and produce that inward acceleration
— which in real situations is supplied by tension, gravity, friction, or
a normal force, depending on the setup. There is nothing pulling
"outward" on the orbiting object itself; the outward pull you feel in a
turning car is ==fictitious==, an artifact of analyzing motion from inside ^card-8pkx
the rotating, non-inertial frame, and it vanishes entirely once you
switch to an inertial frame outside the car.

> [!card] mcq
> A ball on a string is swung in a horizontal circle. What force supplies
> the centripetal force that keeps it moving in a circle?
> - [x] The tension in the string, directed toward the center — there is no separate "centripetal force" beyond whichever real force points inward
> - [ ] A distinct centripetal force generated by the circular motion itself, in addition to the string's tension
> - [ ] The centrifugal force pushing the ball outward, balanced against tension
> - [ ] Gravity, since it is the only force acting on the ball besides tension ^card-aa61

Why is calling something "the centripetal force" different from naming a force like gravity or tension — what category of thing is it? :: It's a role, not a distinct type of force with its own source. "Centripetal" describes the direction and effect of whatever net real force (tension, gravity, friction, a normal force, or some combination) happens to be pointing toward the center of the circular path; it never appears as an extra term added on top of the real forces already in a free-body diagram. ^card-0lj2

Period `T` (time for one full revolution) and frequency `f` (revolutions
per unit time) are reciprocals, and both relate to angular velocity:

```
T = 1/f
omega = 2*pi/T = 2*pi*f
v = omega*r
```

A car rounding a flat, unbanked curve of radius `r` relies entirely on
static friction to supply the centripetal force, and that friction is
capped at `mu_s*N`. Setting the maximum available friction equal to the
required centripetal force gives the fastest speed the car can take the
curve without sliding:

```
mu_s*m*g = m*v^2/r
v_max = sqrt(mu_s*g*r)
```

On a banked curve, the roadway is tilted so that a component of the
**normal** force itself points toward the center, reducing or (at the
design speed) eliminating the need for friction entirely — which is
exactly why icy banked curves can still be taken safely near their
design speed while icy flat curves cannot.

> [!card] mcq
> Two identical curves have the same radius: curve A is flat, curve B is
> banked and icy (essentially zero friction). At the banked curve's
> design speed, what supplies the centripetal force in each case?
> - [x] Curve A: static friction alone. Curve B: a component of the normal force alone, requiring no friction at the design speed
> - [ ] Both rely entirely on friction, since only friction can point toward the center of a curve
> - [ ] Curve A: normal force. Curve B: friction, since banking removes the normal force's horizontal component
> - [ ] Neither needs any force, because uniform circular motion requires zero net force ^card-c56f

At the top of a vertical loop, gravity points toward the center (downward,
which is inward at that point of the loop). The minimum speed to maintain
contact with the track is the speed at which gravity alone, with the
track supplying zero normal force, exactly equals the required
centripetal force:

```
m*g = m*v_min^2/r
v_min = sqrt(g*r)
```

Why does v_min = sqrt(g*r) represent the *minimum* speed at the top of a vertical loop rather than some other special value? :: Below that speed, gravity alone would be more than what's needed to supply the centripetal force at that radius, which is impossible for the track to compensate for (a track can only push, via normal force, never pull) — so the object would fall away from the circular path. At v_min, gravity exactly supplies the required centripetal force with the normal force at zero; at any higher speed, the track adds a positive normal force on top of gravity to supply the extra centripetal force needed. ^card-q4sb

Everything so far assumes uniform circular motion — constant speed. When
speed also changes along the circular path, there is a second,
**tangential** component of acceleration (along the direction of motion,
`a_t = dv/dt`) in addition to the centripetal component, and the two are
perpendicular: centripetal acceleration changes the direction of motion,
tangential acceleration changes its speed.

Why must the centripetal and tangential components of acceleration be treated as perpendicular contributions rather than added as scalars, for an object speeding up while moving along a circular path? :: Because they change different, independent aspects of the velocity vector — centripetal acceleration is always perpendicular to velocity and changes only its direction, while tangential acceleration is parallel to velocity and changes only its magnitude (speed). Since they point in perpendicular directions, the total acceleration is their vector sum, not a simple scalar sum of magnitudes. ^card-ctdx
